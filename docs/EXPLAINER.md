# Fidelis — Explainer

A refresher for explaining Fidelis to recruiters. Plain English first, key technical terms kept.

> **Status:** early development. The Python environment and a smoke test of every core library exist (`docs/environment.md`). All five pass on the dev laptop and on L40S and A100 GPUs on USC's CARC cluster, run through the same Snakemake workflow. The workflow also builds the Jezero test scene: it downloads the USGS data, cuts a 10 km tile around the landing site, makes the CTX-like matching map, and turns the tile into a textured 3D terrain mesh at 4 m detail, in true ground metres (checks in `docs/benchmarks/terrain-mesh.md`). Around that tile sits a coarser 20 m ring built from the USGS CTX data, which makes a scene about 26 km across, blended at the join and bent to Mars's curvature. That lets the simulated camera tilt up to 18° and drift ±2.75 km instead of 3° and ±400 m (`docs/benchmarks/wide-scene.md`). A path-traced renderer draws that mesh as the descent camera would see it, from any pose and under any sun. From the 4.2 km and 2.2 km altitudes where Perseverance's vision system took its pictures, a 1024 × 1024 image at 64 samples per pixel takes a median 0.053–0.058 s on the laptop GPU and uses about 2.2 GB (2,102 MiB) of its memory, once its GPU code is compiled (the first render in a fresh process took 0.60 s; only nadir views on one GPU measured; `docs/benchmarks/mesh-render.md`). The navigator exists too: it matches a descent image against the 6 m CTX-like map and solves for the camera's position. In perfect conditions (straight down, north up, overhead sun, no noise) it locates within 0.6–1.9 m from both ends of that altitude range, on one test position. Given the camera's attitude, as Perseverance knew its own from its IMU, it first warps each photo to north-up, so turned views locate as well as north-up ones: 1.62–1.86 m at 2.2 km for headings of 15°, 45°, 90° and 180°, with a perfectly known attitude (`docs/benchmarks/navigator.md`). A Gaussian splat scene of Jezero, trained on the cluster from mesh renders under the landing-day sun, now stands in for the mesh behind the simulation interface: it matches held-out mesh renders to at worst 32.6 dB PSNR and 0.978 SSIM, and on the same 20 descents its navigation errors track the mesh's closely (`docs/benchmarks/splat.md`). Plain Monte Carlo runs on the cluster too. The latest scan found no failures in 300 descents (`docs/benchmarks/failure-scan.md`), so realistic error sources are now being added one at a time (ADR 0003). Everything else below describes the *planned* system from `docs/fidelis-spec.md`.

## The two-minute pitch

**The problem.** Mars landers navigate the last stretch of descent by photographing the ground and matching it against an orbital map. This is called terrain-relative navigation (TRN), and Perseverance used it at Jezero Crater. Before a landing, the team needs to know how often that matching fails. You can't test-land on Mars, so the answer has to come from thousands of simulated descents. Realistic simulations are expensive, though, and cheap ones are biased.

**What Fidelis does.** It builds a photoreal 3D model of Jezero from public NASA/USGS data and flies thousands of randomized simulated descents over it. On each one it runs a learned image-matching navigator and counts how often the navigator ends up too far off. To keep that affordable, it mixes many cheap, low-quality renders with a few expensive, high-quality ones. That technique is multifidelity Monte Carlo, run with NASA Langley's MXMCPy library. Then it proves the cheaper estimate is right by checking it against a brute-force answer.

**Why it's interesting.** It ties together three skills on one problem NASA and JPL actually have: 3D reconstruction (Gaussian splatting), deep learning (learned feature matching), and uncertainty quantification (multifidelity estimation and reliability analysis).

## How it works

1. **Build the scene.** Download a USGS elevation map (DTM) and orthoimage of Jezero, then turn them into a textured 3D terrain mesh.
2. **Make it fast to render.** Train Gaussian splat scenes from mesh renders, one per sun angle, as a faster stand-in for the mesh.
3. **Fly descents.** Randomize position, altitude, camera tilt, noise, blur, exposure, and sun angle, then render what the descent camera would see.
4. **Navigate.** ALIKED finds keypoints, LightGlue matches them to the orbital map, and PnP solves for position. Record how far off the estimate is.
5. **Estimate failure.** Use MXMCPy to combine cheap and expensive fidelity levels and estimate how often the navigator misses by more than 40 m, measured against the map (ADR 0003). The levels are cheaper versions of the navigator, since matching costs far more than rendering (ADR 0002). The first try at building them, map thinning, gave no usable cheap level, so that's still open.
6. **Prove it.** Compare the result against a brute-force high-fidelity reference, repeated over many independent trials.
7. **Show it.** A web viewer replays descents over the terrain and shows landing-estimate scatter building up. The centerpiece is the lander camera's view, with the matcher's keypoints and map matches drawn live, so you can watch the network work out where it is.

## Key concepts

<!-- Format: **Term** — plain-English meaning. *In Fidelis:* why it matters here. -->

**Lander Vision System (LVS) and the CTX map** — the camera-based navigation Perseverance used at Jezero. It matched descent photos against an orbital map built from MRO's Context Camera (CTX), which is much coarser than the descent photos. *In Fidelis:* the navigator matches against the real thing, USGS's CTX mosaic of Jezero, which USGS calls the LVS map (#36). Before that it matched a CTX-like stand-in, made by averaging down the photo the scene renders. LVS had to know its position within 40 m, 99% of the time, and that requirement is now our failure line (ADR 0003). We measure error against the map, as LVS did, so a whole-map shift isn't counted as a navigation failure.

**Pilot study** — a small trial run that measures each fidelity level's cost and how closely the levels track each other (their covariance). MXMCPy treats those measurements as exact and uses them to split the sample budget and to predict its own accuracy. *In Fidelis:* a pilot that's too small makes MXMCPy overconfident. Its predicted variance was off by about 100% at 10 pilot samples, even on an easy test case (MXMCPy NASA report, Table 3), and small pilots tend to bias it low (Stanley et al. 2026). Rare failures make this worse, so our pilot grows in stages until its conclusions stop changing. See `docs/research/pilot-study-sizing.md`.

**Keypoints, matches, and RANSAC inliers** — a keypoint is a distinctive spot in an image (a crater rim, a boulder) that a neural network (ALIKED) picks out. A match pairs a keypoint in the descent photo with the same spot on the orbital map (LightGlue). Some matches are wrong, so RANSAC keeps only the group of matches that agree on one camera position. Those are the inliers, and the rest are rejected as outliers. *In Fidelis:* inlier count is the first clue to why a descent failed. Drawing matches, with inliers and outliers in different colors, is both the main debugging view and the project's headline visual.

**JIT (just-in-time) compilation** — building code into machine instructions the first time it's needed, on the user's own machine, instead of shipping it prebuilt. *In Fidelis:* gsplat compiles its GPU code the first time it renders (minutes, not seconds), so its build has to work on every machine we run on. That's where most of the Windows setup trouble came from.

**Pinning and forking dependencies** — pinning means locking a library to one exact version or commit so every machine installs identical code. Forking means keeping your own copy of a library to carry fixes upstream hasn't released yet. *In Fidelis:* `uv.lock` pins everything; MXMCPy and gsplat are pinned to GitHub commits, and gsplat comes from our fork until its Windows fixes are released. A fork is a maintenance cost, so the docs say exactly when to drop it.

**SLURM, Snakemake, and profiles**: SLURM is the job queue on a shared computing cluster. You ask it for resources (one GPU of a given model, some memory, a time limit, and which account to bill), and it runs your job when they're free. Snakemake is a workflow manager: you write each step as a rule saying what it needs, and Snakemake works out what to run and submits it. A profile is a settings file that says *where* the rules run. *In Fidelis:* the same rules run on the laptop GPU (`profiles/local`) or on USC's CARC cluster (`profiles/carc`) without changing the workflow. The cluster profile always names the billing account (otherwise the SLURM plugin guesses, and could bill another group) and pins the GPU model, because cost measurements are only comparable on one GPU type.

**Why failure flags correlate worse than errors** — turning a continuous error into a pass/fail flag throws information away, and a biased cheap model puts its pass/fail cutoff in a different place from the expensive one. *In Fidelis:* a synthetic prototype showed this clearly: cheap and expensive errors that tracked each other closely gave pass/fail flags that agreed much less. That is why Fidelis plans a smoothed (sigmoid) failure indicator, and why the pilot study measures correlation on the failure quantity itself. It still needs confirming on real renders.

**DTM, orthoimage, and the matching map**: a DTM (digital terrain model) is a height map, one elevation per pixel. An orthoimage is an overhead photo corrected so every pixel lands where it belongs on the map. *In Fidelis:* USGS's Jezero DTM (1 m pixels) and orthoimage (0.25 m pixels, from HiRISE) are the ground truth for the test scene. The navigator doesn't match against that sharp photo, though. Perseverance's Lander Vision System matched against a map from the coarser CTX camera, about 6 m per pixel, so Fidelis first averaged the orthoimage down 24× to match. A sharper map than the real one would make the navigator look better than it is. That averaged map was a stand-in: since #36 the navigator matches against the real CTX mosaic, with heights from the CTX DTM, and the averaged map is kept only so earlier results can be rerun.

**Checksums and pinning data**: a checksum (here SHA-256) is a short fingerprint of a file's exact bytes. Change one byte and the fingerprint changes. *In Fidelis:* USGS doesn't publish checksums for the Jezero mosaics, so the first download records its own, and every later download on any machine has to match it. That catches a truncated download or a file quietly replaced upstream. The catch is that it trusts the first download ("trust on first use").

**Textured mesh, UV coordinates, and float32 precision**: a terrain mesh is a surface made of triangles, one corner (vertex) per height sample. A texture is an image draped over it. Each vertex carries a UV coordinate, its position in the image from 0 to 1 across and up, which says which part of the image paints that spot. GPUs store positions as float32, which keeps about 7 significant digits. *In Fidelis:* the Jezero mesh has a vertex every 4 m (6.27 million of them over 10 km), each one the average of 16 of the DTM's 1 m pixels, and it matches that averaged DTM exactly at its samples. It's built around the tile centre: in raw map coordinates (around 4.6 million metres east), float32 could only place vertices to the nearest half metre.

**Path tracing and samples per pixel**: path tracing makes an image by following light rays backwards from the camera into the scene, bouncing off surfaces, and checking at each hit whether the sun is visible. Shadows come out right because they're computed, not painted on. Each pixel averages many random rays (samples per pixel, spp); fewer samples is faster but grainier. *In Fidelis:* Mitsuba 3 path-traces the terrain mesh. At 64 spp a descent image takes a median 0.053–0.058 s on the laptop once warmed up, and is within 0.9–2.2% (RMS) of a 1024-spp render on the 10 km mesh. With the same random seed the image repeats to within 1e-6.

**Map projections and the east–west stretch**: a map projection flattens a sphere onto a grid of metres, and no flat grid keeps every distance right. The USGS Jezero maps use an equirectangular projection, which keeps every line of latitude as long as the equator. Away from the equator that stretches east–west distances by 1/cos(latitude). *In Fidelis:* at Jezero (18.4° N) one map metre east–west is 0.949 ground metres, so the first terrain mesh was 5.4% too wide east–west, with east–west slopes too gentle and shadows cast east or west too long. The mesh now multiplies east–west distances by cos(latitude) and records the factor, so poses and distances are in true ground metres.

**Area averaging (downsampling)**: making an image coarser by replacing each block of pixels with their average, the way a camera pixel collects all the light falling on it. *In Fidelis:* the 10 km tile is four times coarser than the raw elevation data (4 m) and eight times coarser than the raw photo (2 m), because a full-detail 10 km mesh filled the laptop GPU. At the 4.2–2.2 km altitudes the descent camera images from, one of its pixels covers 4.3–8.2 m of ground, so the coarser tile is no coarser than what the camera sees (the 4 m mesh is about one height sample per camera pixel at 2.2 km).

**Camera pose and intrinsics**: a pose says where a camera is and which way it points (a position plus a rotation). Intrinsics say how it maps directions to pixels: focal length and the principal point, where the optical axis hits the image. *In Fidelis:* the renderer, splat training and the navigator all have to mean the same thing by a pose, or a perfect navigator would still report errors. Everything uses OpenCV's convention (x right, y down, z forward), written down in `docs/camera-conventions.md`. The descent camera copies Perseverance's LCAM: 1024 × 1024 pixels, 90° field of view (Maki et al. 2020).

**Seeded random sampling**: a computer's "random" numbers come from a formula started by a number called the seed; the same seed always produces the same numbers. *In Fidelis:* every simulated descent draws its conditions (position, altitude, tilt, heading, noise, blur, exposure, sun angle) from a recorded seed, so any single descent can be regenerated exactly to inspect why it failed. Each condition gets its own random stream, keyed by the seed and the condition's name, so changing how tilt is drawn doesn't silently reshuffle every other condition of an existing seed.

**PnP (Perspective-n-Point)**: given a handful of spots whose 3D positions you know and where each one appears in a photo, PnP works out where the camera must have been and which way it pointed. *In Fidelis:* each matched map keypoint becomes a 3D point (its map position plus the terrain height beneath it), and OpenCV's PnP inside RANSAC turns the matches into the navigator's position fix. Fewer than 10 agreeing matches counts as a failed solve, recorded rather than crashing the run.

**Scale and rotation invariance**: a feature detector is scale-invariant if it finds the same spot whether the image is zoomed in or out, and rotation-invariant if it does so when the image is turned. ALIKED and LightGlue are neither, much. *In Fidelis:* matching held up from a map about twice as sharp as the descent image to one almost 3× coarser, but broke when the map was 4× sharper (`docs/benchmarks/navigator.md`). Turning the camera 90° from north-up broke it too. Perseverance avoided that by rotating each photo using its known attitude before matching, and Fidelis now does the same (see *Homography*).

**Homography**: a 3×3 matrix that maps every pixel of one flat picture to another, the maths behind "straighten this photo". When a camera only turns (it doesn't move), its old and new views are related exactly by one, whatever the ground looks like. *In Fidelis:* the navigator uses the attitude the spacecraft believes it has to compute the homography to a straight-down, north-up view of the same spot, warps the photo with it, matches, then maps the matched points back to the original photo's pixels. That's how a matcher that can't handle rotation still works at any heading, and it removes tilt in the same step.

**Knowledge error vs true dispersion**: two different ways a lander can be "off". True dispersion is where the vehicle actually is, compared with where it was aimed (Perseverance: about 1.1 km standard deviation before flight). Knowledge error is how wrong the vehicle's *own belief* about its position is (designed for up to 3.2 km; 137 m in flight). *In Fidelis:* the scenario sampler draws the true position, because that decides what ground the camera sees. Knowledge error only matters if the navigator narrows its search around a prior guess, which it doesn't yet.

**The simulation interface (a "seam")**: one function that everything else talks to. Give it one scenario (a seed's random conditions) and a fidelity level, and it returns a record: where the camera really was, where the navigator thought it was, the error between them, how many matches and inliers backed the fix, how long it took and on which GPU. *In Fidelis:* the Monte Carlo layer, the tests and the match pictures all go through `Simulator.run`, so swapping the renderer (the path-traced mesh, or a Gaussian splat scene) changes nothing above it. A navigator that gives up returns a failure record instead of crashing the run.

**Gaussian splatting**: a way to represent a 3D scene as millions of small, soft, coloured blobs (3D Gaussians), each with a position, size, orientation and opacity. Rendering just projects the blobs onto the image and blends them, which a GPU does very fast. The blobs are fitted to photos of the scene by gradient descent: render, compare with the photo, nudge every blob, repeat, splitting blobs where detail is missing and deleting useless ones ("densification"). *In Fidelis:* the "photos" are mesh renders from descent-camera poses under one sun, so the splat bakes that sun's lighting in, which is why there's one splat scene per sun. Because the terrain's true shape is known, the blobs start on the mesh's own vertices instead of from scratch. gsplat does the rendering and training.

**PSNR and SSIM (image-quality metrics)**: two standard scores for how close one image is to a reference. PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio) measures the average pixel error in decibels relative to the brightest value; every 10 dB means 10× less squared error, and 30 dB is an average error of about 3% of full brightness. SSIM (structural similarity) compares local brightness, contrast and texture patterns, from 1 (identical) down; it notices blur that PSNR forgives. *In Fidelis:* the splat is checked against mesh renders from held-out poses (views it never trained on), relative to the camera's full scale. The bar, 30 dB and 0.9 in every view, is our provisional choice. Every report also scores a constant grey image, so a bar that a blank picture could clear would be obvious.

**Held-out data**: examples kept out of training, used only to check the result, so the check measures how well a model generalizes rather than how well it memorized. *In Fidelis:* the splat's training views, its held-out views and every Monte Carlo descent use separate seed ranges, so no descent the failure estimate depends on renders a pose the splat trained on.

**Camera model: exposure, motion blur, sensor noise**: a renderer produces perfect light; a real camera smears it while the shutter is open (motion blur), scales it by how long the shutter was open (exposure), adds electronic noise, and clips anything brighter than its maximum. *In Fidelis:* every render goes through those steps with the scenario's own blur, exposure error and noise level, and the noise is drawn from the scenario's seed so a rerun is identical. Perseverance's camera had no auto-exposure, so "exposure gain" is how far off the pre-set exposure was.

**Seams between data sources (blending and stitching)**: when two maps of different sharpness meet, three things can go wrong at the join: a crack (a gap between the two 3D surfaces), a step (their heights disagree), and a visible edge (their brightness differs). *In Fidelis:* the sharp HiRISE tile sits inside a coarser CTX ring. A strip of triangles reuses the tile's exact edge vertices, so the two surfaces share every edge and no ray can slip between them. Heights and brightness fade from CTX to HiRISE across a 200 m band, so neither steps. What blending can't hide is that the ring is softer, because the CTX data simply has less detail.

**Curvature drop (d² / 2R)**: a flat map of a round planet is wrong by how far the ground curves away below a flat plane: about d² / 2R at distance d, for planet radius R. *In Fidelis:* 3.7 m at 5 km, which didn't matter on the 10 km tile, but about 25 m at 13 km, the edge of the wide scene. Both the rendered terrain and the navigator's map points now drop by it.

**Image registration (phase correlation) and the whole-map offset**: registration means finding how far one image must slide to line up with another. Phase correlation does it with Fourier transforms: it keeps only the *pattern* of edges and textures (not brightness), so two photos taken under different light can still be lined up, to a fraction of a pixel. *In Fidelis:* the real LVS map (from CTX) and the scene we render (from HiRISE) are two different products, so they may sit slightly apart as a whole. We measure that shift once by registering the two images in 169 tiles across the 10 km area, and subtract it before judging a landing (ADR 0003's "map frame"). Whatever still differs tile to tile is local map distortion, and that stays in the error, because a real navigator would suffer it too.

**Two-map testing (the map isn't the scene)**: if a simulator draws its camera images from the same map the navigator matches against, the two agree on every pixel and matching is unrealistically easy. The fix is to render from one source and match against another, made from different photos. JPL tested LVS this way with two CTX-based maps. *In Fidelis:* the scene renders from HiRISE, and the navigator matches against the real CTX map, its picture and its heights both (#36). Nothing HiRISE-derived reaches the navigator's map, and a test builds the map with the HiRISE files deleted to prove it. The true camera position still comes from the scene, never from the navigator's map, so the map's height errors count against the navigator as they would in flight.

**Confidence interval for a failure rate (Wilson interval)**: with 1 failure in 20 tries, "5%" alone hides how unsure that is. A confidence interval gives the range of true rates the data can't rule out. The textbook formula breaks for rare events: zero failures gives an interval of exactly [0, 0], which claims certainty from 20 samples. Wilson's interval doesn't: zero failures in 20 still allows up to about 16%. *In Fidelis:* every plain Monte Carlo estimate is reported with a 95% Wilson interval.

## Challenges and how we solved them

<!-- Format: ### Title — then: the problem, how we noticed, what we did, what it taught us. -->

### Getting five GPU libraries to agree on one brand-new GPU

**The problem.** The dev laptop has an RTX 5070, a Blackwell-generation GPU new enough that much of the scientific Python stack hadn't caught up. Fidelis needs PyTorch, Mitsuba, gsplat, LightGlue, and NASA's MXMCPy all working in one environment, and two of them broke.

**How we noticed.** The first ticket was a smoke test: one command that makes each library do a small piece of real GPU work and checks the answer against something known (a CPU result, a known camera projection, a known image warp). An import-only check would have missed both failures below.

**What we did.** MXMCPy's PyPI release is from 2020 and calls a PyTorch function that no longer exists. NASA had fixed it on GitHub in 2022 but never published the fix, so we install that exact commit instead. gsplat was harder. It compiles its own CUDA code on first use, and it's developed mostly on Linux, so on Windows with the new CUDA 13 its build failed five different ways, one after another. Every fix already existed, but scattered: two in gsplat's unreleased main branch, and the rest in two separate pull requests from outside contributors that hadn't been merged. We forked gsplat, combined the pieces on one branch (after testing the last fix locally before publishing anything), and pinned Fidelis to that exact commit until an official release catches up. Two more surprises: gsplat throws away its whole compiled cache whenever its build settings change, and on Windows PyTorch needs the Microsoft compiler on *every* load, not just the first build. So Fidelis now loads gsplat through one small function that pins the settings and finds the compiler itself. We also switched off a gsplat module for fisheye-style cameras that we don't use, because its files were taking hours to compile. We also picked the PyTorch build whose CUDA version exactly matches the laptop's CUDA toolkit, which heads off the most common way gsplat's build fails.

**What it taught us.** Test that a library *works*, not that it *imports*. Check upstream before patching: every fix we needed already existed, just unreleased. And when you must carry fixes, pin them to an exact commit and write down when to drop them.

### A cluster job that waited hours to fail in seconds

**The problem.** On the university cluster, a GPU job waits in a shared queue, sometimes for hours, before it runs. The first cluster smoke test waited its turn in the queue, ran for 40 seconds, and failed one of its five checks: gsplat found no CUDA compiler.

**How we noticed.** The job log had a one-line module-system error near the top: the CUDA module "exists but cannot be loaded as requested". It needed two other modules loaded first. Snakemake runs a job's command even when its module load fails, so the job carried on without the compiler instead of stopping.

**What we did.** `module spider` named the missing prerequisites, and we checked the corrected module line on the login node (no GPU, no queue) before resubmitting. Then we made that check automatic: a `preflight` step that Snakemake runs on the login node before submitting any GPU job, with the same modules. It confirms the CUDA compiler is there, matches PyTorch's CUDA version, and can compile a tiny kernel. If it fails, nothing is submitted. The next run got further and hit two new walls inside gsplat's build: a line in our own Windows fork that Linux's compiler rejects, and a system Python missing its C headers. We reproduced the first in a Linux environment on the laptop before touching the cluster again, and added a header check to the preflight for the second. Instead of spending another GPU slot to test the fix, we compiled gsplat on a CPU-only debug node, which gets through the queue in minutes: the build doesn't need a GPU as long as you tell it which chip to target. The next GPU job reused that build and passed all five checks in 38 seconds.

**What it taught us.** Anything checkable without the expensive resource should be checked before asking for it. Our docs had already warned about exactly this risk, and we submitted anyway, so a warning in a doc isn't a check. A step that runs every time is.

### Two programs that disagree about which way is up in an image

**The problem.** The terrain mesh needs to work in two places: Mitsuba, the renderer that makes all of Fidelis's training images, and ordinary 3D viewers, which we use to eyeball it. A texture coordinate of v = 0 means "bottom row of the image" to MeshLab, Blender and three.js, but "top row" to Mitsuba's texture lookup. Pick either convention and one of them shows the terrain with its texture mirrored north to south: craters in the picture land on flat ground in the geometry. Nothing crashes, and a quick look might not catch it, because Mars terrain looks plausible either way.

**How we noticed.** Before writing the mesh code, we gave Mitsuba a two-pixel image and a hand-made triangle and asked which pixel it sampled at a known coordinate. Its PLY loader keeps coordinates as written, and its textures count rows from the top.

**What we did.** The mesh file follows the standard convention (v = 0 at the bottom), so viewers display it correctly. The one function that hands the mesh to Mitsuba flips v. Tests check registration through Mitsuba's own texture lookup, on a fake image where each pixel's value encodes its own row and column, so the value Mitsuba returns says exactly where it looked. On the real tile, a straight-down render matches the orthoimage: correlation 0.972, and no shift that phase correlation can measure at 2 m pixels (it resolves a fraction of a pixel, not centimetres). Mirroring the render north–south drops the correlation to −0.109. See `docs/benchmarks/terrain-mesh.md`.

**What it taught us.** When two tools share a file format, check the convention each one reads, not the one the format spec implies. And test through the consumer that actually matters (here, the renderer), on data built so a mistake can't look plausible.

### Making the renderer and the navigator agree on what a camera pose means

**The problem.** Fidelis renders descent images with Mitsuba and later asks the navigator (OpenCV's PnP) to recover the camera position from them. If the two disagree about a pose by even a flipped axis or half a pixel, the navigator gets "wrong" answers that are really a bookkeeping error, and the failure estimate inherits it. Mitsuba's camera frame isn't OpenCV's: it points +x left and +y up, and counts pixel centres from 0.5 instead of 0.

**How we noticed.** Before trusting the conversion, a test renders small glowing spheres from a tilted, rolled camera with its optical centre deliberately off the image centre, and requires each sphere's image to land within 0.1 pixel of where OpenCV's own projection says it should. The first run missed by up to 14.4 pixels in a telltale way: every sphere was mirrored about the image centre by exactly the principal-point offset. Mitsuba's `principal_point_offset` setting runs the opposite way to pixel coordinates.

**What we did.** Flipped the sign, and the test now holds to 0.1 pixel for all three spheres. The conventions are written down in `docs/camera-conventions.md` so splat training and the navigator reuse them rather than re-deriving them.

**What it taught us.** Check a convention against the other library's own code (here `cv2.projectPoints`), not against your reading of its docs. And make the test geometry asymmetric, with an off-centre principal point and a rolled camera, so that a mirrored or swapped convention can't pass by symmetry.

### A tile too small, a map too wide, and a render that failed without an error

**The problem.** The first Jezero tile was 2 km across. The descent camera's 90° view sees twice its altitude across, so above about 1 km it saw past the edge of the terrain. Perseverance's Lander Vision System took its pictures from 4.2 km down to 2.2 km, entirely above that. Separately, the mesh was 5.4% too wide east–west.

**How we noticed.** Building the renderer meant picking test altitudes, and the 2 km tile only fit views below 1 km. That sent us to NASA/JPL's landing press kit for the real altitudes. While working out how big a tile we'd need, converting the map's coordinates back to latitude and longitude showed that 1000 map metres east–west is only 948.6 m of ground at Jezero. Then, trying a 10 km tile at 2 m detail, the laptop GPU filled up and Mitsuba returned images with streaked rows and a black wedge, without raising any error.

**What we did.** The tile is now 10 km, the most valid data centred on the landing site allows, averaged down to 4 m geometry and a 2 m photo texture, no coarser than a descent-camera pixel at those heights. It renders in about 0.055 s per image using about 2.2 GB of GPU memory. The mesh multiplies east–west distances by cos(latitude), and its registration check now compares in ground metres: the render matches the photo with correlation 0.978, against 0.529 if the photo is left uncorrected. Every benchmark render now goes through a check that fails on any row or column of exact zeros. That catches a render that goes black along whole lines, and a view that runs off the edge of the terrain. We never ran it on the corrupt 2 m render itself, so whether it would have caught that one is untested.

**What it taught us.** Check the operating envelope (here, the real mission altitudes) before sizing the test scene. Never assume map units are ground units. And when a GPU tool can fail silently, add a cheap check on its output instead of trusting the absence of an error.

### A map too small for a tilting camera

**The problem.** Each simulated descent tilts the camera and shifts it sideways at random, as Perseverance's did on its parachute. But the terrain only exists inside our 10 km tile, so a view that reaches past the edge renders black ground, and the navigator would "fail" because the map ran out, not because navigation is hard. That would quietly inflate the failure probability.

**How we noticed.** The sampler refuses any configuration whose worst-case draw could see past the tile. Working that case through properly (the image corner reaches about 1.4 times farther than its edge, the camera can turn so a corner points outward, and lower ground widens the view) left only a few hundred metres of slack, not the 2.5 km a straight-down estimate suggested. Research into the real descent then showed Perseverance's camera swung about −12° to +5° off vertical, and pre-flight simulations averaged 7–18°. Our map fits 3°.

**What we did.** We shipped the sampler with tilt capped at 3° and offsets at ±400 m, labelled in the config as limits of the map, not of Mars, and kept the researched values next to them. We checked whether a bigger map exists: the sharp HiRISE data allows at most about 12.4 km even off-centre (measured on a 20 m grid, so an upper bound), but USGS's coarser CTX terrain covers a hole-free 27.7 km square on the same grid. Its photo is the map Perseverance's vision system actually carried. Its heights differ from HiRISE's by a median of 0.5 m, but individual spots differ by several metres (5th–95th percentile −5.8 to +7.1 m), so the boundary needs blending. Building a wider scene with a CTX outer ring was its own ticket (#33), and it has now landed: tilt goes to 18° and offsets to ±2.75 km (see the next story).

**What it taught us.** Check the worst case, not the typical one, and check it with the real geometry: the corner of the image, not its edge. And keep distributions in config with their sources beside them, so a constraint imposed by our tooling is visible as such rather than mistaken for physics.

### A coarse map that wasn't the problem, and a library that quietly shrank it

**The problem.** The project's biggest early worry was that the 6 m CTX-like map would be too blurry to match against sharp descent images, the way it is for the real mission. We needed to know where matching breaks before building anything on top of it.

**How we noticed.** Two surprises. First, LightGlue's ALIKED wrapper shrinks every image to 1024 pixels by default, so our 1670-pixel map would have been matched at about 10 m per pixel without any warning; reading its source caught it before any number was taken. Second, extracting features from the sharpest test map ran the 8 GB laptop GPU out of memory: the network builds a 128-channel copy of the whole image.

**What we did.** Both images are now matched at their own resolution, and the map's features are extracted in 1024-pixel tiles, once per map. Then a sweep from a 2 m map to a 12 m one, at the top and bottom of the altitude range where Perseverance's vision system worked, showed the 6 m map works fine (within 0.92 m, about 2,000 agreeing matches). The one failure was the opposite of the worry: the *sharpest* map against the *highest*, blurriest descent image. What matters is that map and photo show the ground at a similar scale, not that the map is sharp. The same check found a bigger problem waiting: a camera turned 90° from north-up fails to match at all.

**What it taught us.** Read a library's defaults before trusting its output. Measure the risk you're worried about instead of guarding against it in advance (the spec's fallbacks for a too-coarse map turned out unnecessary). And test one step beyond the easy case: the heading check took minutes and found the issue that matters next.

### A navigator that only worked facing north

**The problem.** The matcher (ALIKED + LightGlue) only recognises the ground if the photo faces the same way as the map. Turned 45° from north it missed by 12 m; at 90° and 180° it found nothing. The scenario sampler spins the camera to any heading, so most simulated descents would have failed for that reason alone, and the failure probability would have measured a matcher limitation instead of anything about the navigation.

**How we noticed.** The navigator's first benchmark tried a few headings beyond its "perfect conditions" north-up view, just to see.

**What we did.** Copied what Perseverance did: use the attitude the spacecraft knows (from its IMU) to warp each photo to a straight-down, north-up view before matching, then map the matched points back to the real photo's pixels for the position solve. On the Jezero renders every heading tried now locates as well as north-up (1.62–1.86 m at 2.2 km, against 1.88 m north-up). Two bugs surfaced on the way. A rounding slip made a 90°-turned 256-pixel image 257 pixels tall, with a blank row the detector reacted to. And the first test of the pixel mapping checked the matched points against the navigator's *own* answer, so mapping them back the wrong way round still passed: the position solver simply turned the camera around to agree. Mutation testing (deliberately breaking the code to see if a test notices) caught it; the test now checks against the true camera.

**What it taught us.** Check results against ground truth, not against the system's own answer: a self-consistent check can't see an error the rest of the system absorbs. Also, a fix that resamples an image isn't free: on a small synthetic test, north-up located within 0.174 m but a 15° turn only within 0.795 m, and 45° within 0.496 m. On the Jezero renders the effect is hidden under a larger, still unexplained error of about 1.9 m.

### A cost measurement that was really measuring file loading

**The problem.** Every simulated descent records how long it took, because the multifidelity estimator later decides how many cheap and expensive samples to buy from those costs. A wrong cost means a wrong budget split, silently.

**How we noticed.** The first 20-descent run's costs weren't one cluster: most took about 0.4 s, but five took 1.85–2.73 s. Those five were exactly the first descent under each new sun angle. That's when the renderer loads that sun's copy of the 289 MB terrain mesh, and the loading was happening inside the timer. The code's own comment said the cost excluded loading; the data said otherwise. Comparing the two runs also showed the same seed doesn't give exactly the same error on the GPU (up to 0.54 m apart), which a single run can't reveal.

**What we did.** The renderer now has a separate "load this sun" step that the simulator calls before starting the clock, and a test uses a fake renderer that takes 2 s to load to prove the recorded cost leaves it out. On the rerun, costs were 0.38–0.58 s, except the very first render of the process (0.92 s, probably one-time GPU warm-up). Chasing the repeatability gap stage by stage showed the navigator repeats exactly on a fixed image, but noise of 1e-7, less than the renderer's own jitter, moves its answer by up to 0.518 m. The GPU test that compared errors between two runs had only been passing by luck; it now compares the camera images instead.

**What it taught us.** Look at the spread of a measurement, not just its average: the outliers lined up with an event (a scene load), and that pointed straight at the bug. And run things twice: one run can't show you that it isn't reproducible.

### A wider scene, a wrong fact in our own notes, and a crater rim that wasn't a problem

**The problem.** Build a 26 km scene by putting the coarse CTX terrain around the sharp HiRISE tile, with no visible join, so the sampler can tilt the camera like the real descent did.

**How we noticed.** Two things surprised us. Our own research note said the CTX and HiRISE data share a pixel grid. Reading the downloaded files' headers showed they don't: CTX is offset by 0.75 m and 3.14 m, a fraction of a pixel. Then the wide scene's height range came out at 1203 m, against 259 m on the old tile. Plugged into the sampler's "can a view see off the edge?" check, that would have eaten most of the room the ring was built to give.

**What we did.** The grid offset is handled by sampling CTX smoothly (bilinear) wherever the two meet, and the note now carries a correction. For the height range, we asked what the check actually needs: how much *lower* than the ground under the camera the edge of a view can be. The 1203 m is mostly Jezero's crater rim, which is *higher* ground, and higher ground only shortens a view. The real bound, from the tile's highest point to the scene's lowest, is 351 m. The sampler now also refuses any offset that would take the camera off the tile that number was measured on. The join was tested for cracks by checking that every triangle edge is shared exactly twice, and a GPU render showed no brightness line at the seam. It also showed the ring is visibly softer, which we wrote up as a limitation rather than calling the seam invisible.

**What it taught us.** Re-check inherited facts against the bytes, even our own. And when a number blows up, ask what the check needs it for before accepting the worst case: the conservative bound and the correct bound are not always the same thing.

### A bigger map that no longer fit on the laptop

**The problem.** After the wide scene landed, rerunning the 20-descent Monte Carlo on the laptop crashed with the GPU out of memory, before a single descent finished.

**How we noticed.** The crash was inside LightGlue, the matcher, not the renderer. The GPU was otherwise idle, so nothing else was competing for memory. The matcher compares every keypoint in the descent photo with every keypoint in the whole orbital map, and the wider map has about 7× the area. Counting showed 70,726 map keypoints, up from 14,290. The table of match scores grows with both counts, and with the renderer's ~3 GB already on the 8 GB card, it no longer fit.

**What we did.** We didn't change the matcher to make it fit: shrinking its search or lowering its precision would change the system under test, and the run would no longer be comparable with the first one. Instead we ran the unchanged code on the university cluster, on a 48 GB L40S, through the same Snakemake workflow. Getting there surfaced three cluster gotchas. The compute nodes have no internet, so the matcher's weights have to be downloaded on the login node first. On the login node, the maths library tried to start 32 threads and hit the per-user process limit. And a newly added command wasn't installed, because the environment had only been re-synced when the lock file changed.

**What it taught us.** Growing an input (here, the map) can quietly break a part that wasn't touched. The wide-scene ticket checked render time and memory, but never ran the navigator against the bigger map. Also: when something doesn't fit, move it to bigger hardware before changing the thing you're measuring.

### A navigator that never failed, because it was matching a photo against itself

**The problem.** A failure-rate estimate needs failures. 300 simulated landings under all five suns, including low 25° suns, gave none: the worst landing was 6.7 m off, against a 12 m failure line. A navigator that never fails can't show off an estimator built for rare failures.

**How we noticed.** The errors weren't random. Under each sun, every landing was pushed off by about the same amount in about the same direction, with only about 1 m of wobble. So more landings of the same kind would never cross the line. The tempting fix was to turn up a difficulty dial until it broke, but then the failure rate would be our choice, not a measurement.

**What we did.** We asked what real landings face that ours didn't. A research pass through JPL's papers on Perseverance's Lander Vision System found the gap: our descent images and our map came from the same orbital photo, so every rock and shadow lined up perfectly. The real system matched against a different map, from a different camera, on a different day, under different light. That mismatch was the only documented cause of misses in JPL's field tests. We decided (ADR 0003) to add back only errors that real landings face, sized from sources, one at a time: the real LVS map first, then dust haze, camera calibration and attitude error. We also fixed the failure line at LVS's real 40 m requirement, instead of tuning it until 2–10% of landings fail.

**What it taught us.** A clean result can mean the simulation is too kind, not that the system is reliable. The fix is realism, not difficulty: when you want failures, find out what the real world does that your model doesn't, and never pick the threshold to get the rate you want.

### A measuring tool that liked whole pixels

**The problem.** Measure how far the real LVS map (CTX, 6 m pixels) sits from the HiRISE scene we render, so that shift isn't counted as navigation failure. The answer turned out to be small, about 2 m, so a bias of a few tenths of a metre in the tool would matter.

**How we noticed.** Before trusting the number, we tested the tool on the real images with a *known* fake shift: we moved the CTX file's map coordinates by an exact amount and checked that the measurement moved by the same amount. Shifts of whole 6 m pixels came back exactly. Shifts of a quarter, half or three quarters of a pixel came back up to 0.5 m short, as if the tool were drawn towards whole pixels ("pixel locking"). A synthetic test had passed, and only the real-data check showed it.

**What we did.** The cause was resampling the coarse CTX image onto the HiRISE grid, which smooths it differently depending on where its pixels fall. We flipped it round: CTX stays on its own pixels, and the much finer HiRISE image is averaged over each CTX pixel's footprint instead. The worst injected error fell from 0.51 m to 0.10 m, and the measured offset moved by about 0.2 m on each axis, to +1.99 m east and −1.49 m north (`docs/benchmarks/map-offset.md`). A synthetic test at ¼, ½ and ¾ of a pixel now guards against a repeat. The standard error was also misleading: 0.04–0.08 m, but neighbouring tiles' errors are strongly correlated, so it assumed far more independent evidence than there was. We quote ±0.4 / ±0.6 m from how much the answer changes between halves of the map.

**What it taught us.** Test a measurement tool by planting a known answer in *real* data, not just synthetic data. And when two images have different resolutions, resample the sharp one, never the coarse one.

### A count that changed by 23, and a truth that leaned on the navigator's map

**The problem.** Two loose ends turned up while swapping in the real LVS map (#36). Cluster records said the old map had 70,749 keypoints; our own notes said 70,726. And the simulator placed the *true* camera using the ground height from the navigator's map.

**How we noticed.** The count mismatch had been written down twice as "not investigated". Replacing the map was the moment to find out whether the cluster had a different map. The second one showed up when the navigator's map got its own, coarser heights (CTX's): the truth would have quietly moved with them.

**What we did.** We counted keypoints on one map file, with one version of the code, on two devices: 70,726 on the laptop's RTX 5070 and 70,749 on its CPU, the cluster A40's number. So it is the GPU model's arithmetic, not a different map: a few borderline keypoints tip one way or the other (`docs/benchmarks/ctx-map-scan.md`). For the second, the truth now always reads the scene's own terrain, and a test gives the navigator a map whose heights are wrong on purpose and checks the true camera doesn't move.

**What it taught us.** A small unexplained difference is cheap to chase with a controlled comparison (same file, same code, change one thing). And the truth must never be read through the system under test. It was harmless only while the map and the scene were the same data.

## Headline results

- **The pipeline runs end to end** (`docs/benchmarks/plain-mc.md`). 20 seeded descents over the Jezero mesh all got a fix, with errors of 1.38–6.12 m (median 2.73 m) at about 0.46 s per descent on the laptop GPU. Caveat: that's 0 failures in 20 at a *provisional* 12 m threshold, so the honest failure rate is "somewhere from 0 to 16%" (95% Wilson interval), and the descents are easier than flight. It proves the plumbing, not the reliability. That run used the 10 km tile with tilt capped at 3°.
- **Flight-like tilts and offsets, on the cluster** (`docs/benchmarks/plain-mc.md`). The same 20 seeds on the wide scene, with tilt up to 18° and offsets up to ±2.75 km (the largest drawn: 17.8° and 2.4 km), on one CARC L40S: again every descent got a fix, with errors of 0.76–5.62 m (median 1.63 m) at about 0.25 s per descent. Caveats: still 0 of 20 at the provisional 12 m, so still "0 to 16%". The navigator still gets the true attitude, which undoes tilt before matching. Only 3 of the 20 views saw any of the coarser outer ring (at most about 9% of the image, from a flat-ground approximation), so the ring is barely tested. The low sun (25°) still gives the four largest errors.
- **A Gaussian splat stands in for the mesh** (`docs/benchmarks/splat.md`). Trained in about 10 minutes on one CARC L40S from 300 mesh renders under the landing-day sun, the splat (7.7 million Gaussians) matches 20 held-out mesh renders to at worst 32.6 dB PSNR and 0.978 SSIM; a constant grey image scores 17.8 dB and 0.36. On the same 20 descents under that sun, the splat's navigation errors track the mesh's (correlation 0.93) and run slightly lower (median 2.20 m against 2.37 m), at 0.184 s per descent against 0.234 s. Caveats: one sun, 20 samples, no failures on either at the provisional 12 m, and a quality bar we chose ourselves.
- **No failures in 300 landings under all five suns** (`docs/benchmarks/failure-scan.md`). On one CARC A40 with the mesh renderer, every descent got a fix, and the worst was 6.70 m off. That's 0 of 300 at the pre-registered 12 m, so under 1.3% at 95% confidence. Low (25°) suns roughly double the error (median 4.4–4.8 m against 1.6–2.3 m), but as a steady per-sun offset with about 1 m of scatter, not as occasional bad fixes. Caveats: the map and the renders come from the same photo, and the navigator gets the true attitude, so this is a best case (ADR 0003). The failure line has since moved to 40 m in the map frame.
- **The real LVS map and our scene sit about 2.4 m apart** (`docs/benchmarks/map-offset.md`). Registering the CTX map against the HiRISE tile gives +1.99 m east and −1.49 m north in map metres (±0.4 / ±0.6 m), 6% of the 40 m failure line. One shift doesn't explain everything: after removing it, the rest varies smoothly across the tile, rms 1.15 m and up to about 3.6 m, which stays in the navigation error. Caveats: different sun angles can shift shadows and look like an offset, and that isn't sized. Distortion over less than one 768 m window isn't resolved. The navigator now matches against the CTX map and this offset is removed from every fix (#36). The first scan on that map is planned and not yet run (`docs/benchmarks/ctx-map-scan.md`), so there is no failure rate on the real map yet.
Numbers go here only once they're committed in `docs/benchmarks/` or `docs/adr/`, each with its caveat.

## Honest limitations

<!-- What the results don't show, and why. -->

- **The navigator's map is the public CTX product, not the onboard one, and it's untested so far.** Since #36 the navigator matches against USGS's CTX mosaic with heights from USGS's 20 m CTX DTM. LVS's onboard elevation map was 12 m and built by JPL, so ours is the closest public stand-in. Swapping the map changes two things at once, its picture and its heights, and one scan can't tell them apart. The whole-map offset we remove (2.4 m) was measured over the HiRISE tile; in the outer ring the scene is CTX too, so the true offset there is zero and the correction is slightly wrong for views that reach it. No descent has been scored against this map yet (`docs/benchmarks/ctx-map-scan.md`). If many fail, that may say more about our renders (HiRISE textures relit by a simple sun) than about LVS.

- **The old CTX-like map was an approximation over the tile** (it's kept only to rerun earlier results). Beyond the tile it is the real CTX photo; over the tile it's the HiRISE orthoimage averaged in 24×24 blocks (in two steps: 8×8 into the 2 m texture, then 3×3, rounding to whole grey levels in between). That removes detail CTX can't see, but it doesn't model CTX's optics, noise, or different viewing geometry, and it isn't the real onboard LVS map.

- **The terrain geometry is 4 m, not the DTM's 1 m.** The 10 km mesh averages the 1 m DTM into 4 m cells so it fits on the laptop GPU. Relief smaller than about 4 m exists in the photo texture but not in the geometry, so it doesn't cast its own shadows when relit. A descent-camera pixel at LVS altitudes covers 4.3–8.2 m, but low-sun shadows from small steep features are probably still understated (not measured). The texture also still holds the shadows from the moment HiRISE took the picture; removing those is a separate step (issue #19).

- **Rendered shadows are too dark, and the brightness isn't physical.** The renderer has a sun but no sky, so shadows only get light bounced off nearby terrain; on Mars, dusty sky light fills them in. And the orthoimage's pixel values are tone-matched brightness, not calibrated reflectance, so the renderer's output is relative brightness, not physical radiance.

- **The outer ring is softer than real terrain.** Beyond the 10 km HiRISE tile, the scene is CTX: a 6 m photo on a 20 m mesh. In a straight-down render from 2.2 km, the ring shows about a quarter to a third of the fine detail the tile does (`docs/benchmarks/wide-scene.md`). The camera could resolve more than that, so the matcher likely finds fewer features in the ring, which tilted views see at their far side. The ring's renders and its map also come from the same CTX photo, which flatters matching there, as the tile's shared HiRISE photo already does. How either moves the failure rate isn't measured. The scene ends 12.3 km east–west and 13 km north–south from the centre, which limits the sampler to 18° of tilt; Perseverance's pitch reached about −12°, so that range is covered, but steeper attitudes aren't.

- **The navigator has only been checked in near-perfect conditions.** Straight down, north up, one test position, two suns, pretrained matcher. Those checks used the old map, made from the same photo as the renders, so the two differ only in resolution and shading, which flatters matching. Camera heading is handled by warping with the known attitude, and the simulation assumes that attitude is perfect. A CPU check suggests that's harmless: with the attitude 20° wrong the fix still held, within 1.81 m on a synthetic test, and on Jezero renders a 15° error costs about 0.3 m. Perseverance's real attitude error isn't sourced in the repo; it's expected to be far smaller, which is why it isn't modelled. Position errors also drift between identical runs: on a rerun most results moved by 0.02 m at most, but one 2 m-map result moved from 1.38 to 0.57 m, and how far any result can drift isn't measured (`docs/benchmarks/navigator.md`). The map's terrain heights come from the same 4 m DTM as the rendered terrain, finer than the real onboard elevation map.

- **The "blurry map" risk is retired only for 2 m renders.** The worry was sharp descent photos (from 0.25 m HiRISE detail) against the 6 m map. Our renders only carry the mesh texture's 2 m detail, so the sweep tested a milder gap than the one the spec feared. When sharper renders exist (the splat scenes), the sweep has to be rerun before this counts as settled.

- **"Sun matching the map" is our interpretation.** The map's photo has HiRISE's own lighting baked in, and no sun angle reproduces it exactly. We used an overhead sun, which adds the least shading on top, as the closest stand-in. That's a judgment call, not a literal match.

- **About 1.9 m of the perfect-conditions error is unexplained.** Near 2.2 km the fix sits 1.88 m off; near 4.2 km, 0.63 m. It's far below the 40 m that matters, so it wasn't chased. But it's big enough to hide a half-pixel coordinate bug from a test at that altitude alone (mutation testing caught exactly that), which is why the GPU test checks both ends of the band.

- **One sun has a splat scene so far.** Monte Carlo on the splat holds every descent to the landing-day sun, so it isn't yet the five-sun mix the mesh runs use; the other four are the next ticket's (#20). The quality bar the splat cleared (30 dB, 0.9 in every held-out view) is our own provisional choice, and it passed with 2.6 dB to spare.

- **Swapping in the splat saved less time than expected.** A descent costs 0.184 s on the splat against 0.234 s on the mesh: only 21% less, because roughly 0.18 s of every descent appears to be the navigator, whichever renderer runs (inferred from totals; no per-stage timing yet). Cheaper splat levels can only cut into the remaining ~0.05 s, so the fidelity ladder may also need a cheaper navigator, a fallback the spec already allows.

- **Results so far were scored at a 12 m placeholder.** ADR 0003 fixes failure as a miss beyond 40 m (LVS's requirement), measured against the map, and drops the plan to tune r until 2–10% of descents fail. The code now counts failures that way, with no-fix and beyond-40 m failures reported separately, but every failure rate quoted so far was run at the old 12 m against true position. Those runs are also failure-free at 40 m in the map frame: the map is still made from the scene's own photo, so the map offset is zero, and no error came near 12 m. The real offset arrives with the CTX map (#36, #40). The realistic failure rate may turn out very small. If it does, that is the answer, not a problem to dial away.

- **On the GPU, a seed regenerates its descent's image but not its exact error.** The GPU renderer's output jitters by about a millionth of a pixel's brightness from run to run, and the navigator amplifies that: a change that tiny can decide which keypoints survive. The same seed's error has moved by up to 0.839 m between runs, even within one process. The navigator itself repeats exactly on the same image, and on the CPU the whole pipeline repeats exactly within one process (`docs/benchmarks/plain-mc.md`). That's small against r, but it makes samples right at the threshold partly random, and it limits how closely cheap and expensive fidelity levels can agree.

- **Exposure is set from each image, not from a radiometric prediction.** Perseverance's exposure was fixed in advance from predictions. Our renderer's brightness isn't physical, so the "correct" exposure is taken from the image itself (its brightest 0.1% at full scale), and the scenario's exposure gain is the error on top. That's a stand-in for a prediction that was right before the error, not a model of how the prediction was made.

- **Curvature is modelled; the map's east–west stretch is only approximately.** The scene drops by d² / 2R for Mars's curvature. It still uses one east–west scale for the whole 26 km, which is up to 0.14% (about 17 m) off true shape at the far corners. The rendered terrain and the map share that frame, so the navigator sees a consistent world, just slightly misshapen at its edges.
